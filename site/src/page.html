YieldSpace in Lean

A walk through a Lean 4 proof of eight properties of the constant power sum invariant

Lean 4 and Mathlib · October 2026 · A companion to Niemerg, Robinson and Livnev, YieldSpace: An Automated Liquidity Provider for Fixed Yield Tokens (2020) · Source on GitHub

Abstract

YieldSpace is a formula for pools that trade a fixed yield token (a fyToken) against the asset it pays out at maturity. Its invariant, $x^{1-t} + y^{1-t} = k$, includes the time to maturity $t$, and it makes the interest rate offered by the pool a simple function of the pool's reserves. This page walks through a machine-checked proof, written in Lean 4 with the Mathlib library, of eight properties of the pool's trades. Rates are non-negative when the pool holds at least as much fyToken as base. Every trade has exactly one outcome. Buying raises the price and selling lowers it. Splitting a trade into pieces changes nothing. A round trip loses money when there is a fee. Larger trades get worse prices. Scaling the pool scales its trades. Trading becomes a one-for-one exchange at maturity. Each section states one property, proves it informally, and shows the statement that Lean checked. Writing the statements precisely enough for a proof checker turned up a few places where the paper needs a qualification. Section 6 lists them.

1Introduction

A fyToken is a token that can be redeemed for exactly one unit of a target asset, such as one Dai, on a fixed maturity date. Before that date it usually trades for less than one unit, and the discount is an interest rate. For example, if a fyDai that matures in one year costs $0.95$ Dai, a buyer who holds it to maturity earns $1/0.95 - 1 \approx 5.3\%$ over the year.

Many token exchanges on Ethereum are automated market makers. An automated market maker is a contract that holds reserves of two tokens and trades with anyone. It has no order book. Instead, it accepts any trade that leaves its reserves satisfying a fixed equation, called the invariant, and so the invariant sets the price.

YieldSpace [1] is an invariant designed for fyTokens. The paper derives it, and argues for its properties in the usual informal way. This page checks a set of those properties with a proof assistant: a program that accepts a proof only if every step follows from earlier steps, from definitions, and in the end from the axioms of mathematics. We use Lean 4 [2] and Mathlib [3], Lean's library of formalized mathematics. Mathlib already contains the tools the proofs need: real powers, derivatives, convex functions, the mean value theorem, and limits.

Table 1 lists the eight properties. They are numbered in the order in which they were first written down, as a list of things the formalization should prove. The sections below take them in a different order, so that each proof can use the ones before it.

2Background

2.1Pools defined by an invariant

A pool holds $x$ units of one token and $y$ units of another. A trader who sends $\Delta$ units of the second token into the pool receives some amount of the first. The pool chooses that amount so that the reserves after the trade satisfy the same equation as the reserves before it. That equation is the pool's invariant.

The best-known invariant is the constant product $x \cdot y = k$ used by Uniswap. For example, a pool with $x = y = 100$ has $k = 10\,000$. To take $10$ units of $y$ out of it, a trader must raise $x$ to $10\,000/90 = 111.11$, so the trade costs $11.11$ units of $x$, or $1.111$ per unit of $y$.

Very small trades reveal the price the pool currently offers. If a trade moves the $y$ reserve by $dy$ and the $x$ reserve by $dx$, the price of the $y$ token, measured in units of the $x$ token, is

$$p = -\frac{dx}{dy}. \tag{1}$$

This is the marginal price. Geometrically it is the slope of the curve of possible reserves at the current point, with the sign changed. In the example above the marginal price is $x/y = 1$, while the trade of $10$ units paid $1.111$ per unit. A real trade has a size, and its average price is worse than the marginal price. Section 5.7 makes that precise.

2.2The constant power sum invariant

In YieldSpace, $x$ is the reserve of the base token, the asset that the fyToken pays out at maturity (for example Dai), and $y$ is the reserve of the fyToken. The invariant is

$$x^{1-t} + y^{1-t} = k, \tag{2}$$

where $t$ is the time to maturity, scaled so that $0 < t < 1$ before maturity. In the original deployment $t = 1$ corresponded to four years. When $t = 0$, (2) is the constant sum $x + y = k$, which trades one for one. As $t$ approaches $1$, (2) approaches the constant product (paper, Appendix D). Figure 1 shows curves between these two.

To find the price, differentiate (2) along the curve:

$$(1-t)\,x^{-t}\,dx + (1-t)\,y^{-t}\,dy = 0.$$

By (1), the price of a fyToken in base is then

$$p = -\frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{y^{-t}}{x^{-t}} = \left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{t}. \tag{3}$$

A fyToken bought at price $p$ and held to maturity turns $p$ units of base into $1$. The return over the time that remains is

$$\frac{1}{p} - 1 = \left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^{t} - 1. \tag{4}$$

The paper describes the rate differently. It defines $r$ by $p = (1+r)^{-t}$: $r$ is the rate per unit of $t$, compounded over the time $t$ that remains. Combining this with (3) gives

$$r = \frac{y}{x} - 1. \tag{5}$$

This is the property the formula was designed to have. The rate is the ratio of the reserves minus one, whatever the time to maturity. Both (4) and (5) are positive exactly when $y > x$.

The paper reaches (2) in the opposite direction. It starts from the requirement (5), turns it into the differential equation $-dx/dy = (x/y)^t$, and integrates (paper, §4 and Appendix C). Here we take (2) as given and only need its consequences (3) to (5).

For example, take $x = 100$, $y = 110$ and $t = 0.5$ (two years, if $t = 1$ is four years). The price of a fyToken is $(100/110)^{0.5} = {{price0}}$. A buyer earns ${{rate_mat}}\%$ over the two years to maturity, and the paper's rate is $r = 10\%$ per four years. We use this pool, $(x, y) = (100, 110)$ with $t = 0.5$, as a running example. With $t = 0.5$ the exponent $1 - t$ is $1/2$ and the invariant reads $\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{y} = k$, so most of the numbers below can be checked by hand.

2.3Fees

The paper charges fees in terms of rates rather than prices (paper, §5). A trader who buys fyToken should receive a lower rate than the pool's marginal rate, and a trader who sells fyToken should pay a higher one. The paper does this by replacing $t$ with $gt$ for buys and with $t/g$ for sells, where $0 < g \le 1$ is a fee parameter and $g = 1$ means no fee:

$$\begin{aligned} x^{1-gt} + y^{1-gt} &= k &&\text{(buying fyToken)},\\ x^{1-t/g} + y^{1-t/g} &= k &&\text{(selling fyToken)}. \end{aligned} \tag{6}$$

On the buying curve the marginal price is $(x/y)^{gt}$. When $x < y$, the base $x/y$ is less than $1$, so the smaller exponent $gt$ gives a higher price than $t$ does, as a fee should. In the paper's example, $x = 100$, $y = 110$ and $g = 0.95$, buyers receive a marginal rate of $(110/100)^{0.95} - 1 \approx {{fee_buy_rate}}\%$ instead of $10\%$, and sellers pay $(110/100)^{1/0.95} - 1 \approx {{fee_sell_rate}}\%$. When $x > y$ the comparison reverses, and the fee favors the trader. Section 5.8 comes back to this.

The selling exponent $1 - t/g$ must stay positive, so with a fee we also need $t < g$ (paper, footnote 4).

Two details of Mathlib matter for this project.

Real powers. In Lean, x ^ a with a real exponent $a$ is Mathlib's function Real.rpow. It is defined for every real $x$. For negative $x$, Mathlib sets $x^a = e^{a \log x} \cos(a \pi)$, where $\log x$ means $\log |x|$. This choice is convenient for the library and has no meaning for reserves. So every statement on this page that takes a power carries hypotheses that keep the base non-negative, either directly or through a condition such as $k - z^a \ge 0$.

Checking for gaps. Lean lets a proof be left unfinished with the keyword sorry, and then accepts the statement anyway. The command #print axioms lists everything a theorem depends on in the end. For each of the project's main theorems the answer is [propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound], the three standard axioms that all of Mathlib uses. An unfinished proof anywhere in the chain would add sorryAx to that list. There are none.

4The model in Lean

4.1One exponent for every curve

The fee-free curve and the two fee curves in (6) have the same form, $x^a + y^a = k$, with

$$a = 1 - \tau, \qquad \tau \in \{\, t,\ gt,\ t/g \,\}.$$

In every case $0 < \tau < 1$, so $0 < a < 1$. The project therefore proves almost every result once, for an arbitrary exponent $a$ in $(0, 1)$, and then applies it to each curve. The invariant and its solution for one reserve are:

solve a k z is the reserve $r$ that satisfies $r^a + z^a = k$, namely

$$r = \left(k - z^a\right)^{1/a}. \tag{7}$$

Lean writes $1/a$ as a⁻¹. The formula only makes sense when $k - z^a \ge 0$, and the lemmas about solve assume this. The two basic lemmas say that solve does solve the invariant, and that it is the only non-negative solution:

The proof of eq_solve is a single chain of rewrites. If $r^a + z^a = k$ then $k - z^a = r^a$, and $(r^a)^{1/a} = r$ when $r \ge 0$, which is Mathlib's rpow_rpow_inv.

4.2Trades

A trader can specify a trade in two ways: by the amount sent in, or by the amount taken out. We call these trades with a fixed input and trades with a fixed output. Write the pool's reserves as $(x, y)$, and let the trade change the $y$ reserve by $\Delta$.

  • In outGivenIn a x y Δ the trader sends $\Delta$ of the $y$ token in and receives $$x - \bigl(k - (y + \Delta)^a\bigr)^{1/a}. \tag{8}$$
  • In inGivenOut a x y Δ the trader takes $\Delta$ of the $y$ token out and pays $$\bigl(k - (y - \Delta)^a\bigr)^{1/a} - x. \tag{9}$$

In both, $k = x^a + y^a$ is computed from the reserves just before the trade, as the paper specifies (§4.1).

For example, in the running pool $k = \sqrt{100} + \sqrt{110} = {{k}}$. A trader who sells $10$ fyToken leaves the base reserve at $({{k}} - \sqrt{120})^2 = {{x1_10}}$, and so receives ${{out10}}$ base, an average price of ${{avg10}}$ per fyToken. That is less than the marginal price ${{price0}}$. Section 5.7 shows that it always is.

5The proofs

5.1Non-negative rates (Theorem 1)

A fyToken should not cost more than the base it pays out, since that would mean a negative interest rate. The paper's pool enforces this by checking at the end of each trade that the fyToken price is not above $1$, “or, equivalently, that the fyDai reserves are greater than the Dai reserves” (paper, §6.3). Theorem 1 is the direction of that equivalence the check relies on.

Theorem 1 (Non-negative rates). If $0 < x \le y$ and $t \ge 0$, then the price $(x/y)^t$ is at most $1$, and the rates (4) and (5) are non-negative.
Proof. Since $0 < x/y \le 1$, and a number in $[0, 1]$ raised to a non-negative power stays in $[0, 1]$, we get $p = (x/y)^t \le 1$. Then $1/p - 1 \ge 0$, and $r = y/x - 1 \ge 0$ because $y/x \ge 1$. $\square$

The Lean proof of the price statement was shown in Section 3. The two rate statements are rate_nonneg and paperRate_nonneg.

5.2Well-defined trades (Theorem 4)

A trade is well defined if exactly one outcome preserves the invariant, and the pool still holds a positive amount of each token afterwards. Before proving this we need to know which trade sizes are possible.

Trades with a fixed output. Taking $\Delta$ out of the $y$ reserve is possible exactly when $0 \le \Delta < y$. Then $(y - \Delta)^a \le y^a$, so the quantity inside (9) satisfies

$$k - (y - \Delta)^a \;\ge\; k - y^a = x^a > 0.$$

The new $x$ reserve exists, it is positive, and it is at least $x$, so the trader pays a non-negative amount.

Trades with a fixed input. Here there is a limit that the constant product does not have. Setting $x = 0$ in $x^a + y^a = k$ shows that the curve meets the $y$ axis at $y = k^{1/a}$, as in Figure 1. At that point the pool has paid out all of its base, and a trader who sends in more fyToken has nothing left to receive. For the running pool, $k^{1/a} = {{k}}^2 = {{ymax}}$, so a single sale can bring in at most ${{maxsell}}$ fyToken. The exact condition for a sale of $\Delta$ is

$$(y + \Delta)^a < k, \tag{10}$$

which the project calls Valid.

A simpler sufficient condition is that the trader sends no more than the pool's reserve of the other token, $\Delta \le x$. It follows from the strict subadditivity of $z \mapsto z^a$ when $0 < a < 1$:

$$(x + y)^a < x^a + y^a \qquad (x, y > 0). \tag{11}$$

To prove (11), let $s = x + y$. Since $0 < x/s < 1$ and $a < 1$, we have $(x/s)^a > x/s$, that is, $x^a > (x/s)\, s^a$. In the same way $y^a > (y/s)\, s^a$. Adding the two inequalities gives $x^a + y^a > s^a$. Then, if $\Delta \le x$,

$$(y + \Delta)^a \;\le\; (y + x)^a \;<\; x^a + y^a = k.$$

For the running pool this guarantees that sales of up to $100$ fyToken are possible, while the true limit is ${{maxsell}}$. The bound becomes exact as $t \to 0$, where the pool trades one for one and can pay out at most $x$.

The Lean proof of (11) follows the argument above. The local fact key is the inequality $x^a > (x/s)\, s^a$, proved once for any $u$ with $0 < u < s$, and then used for $u = x$ and $u = y$.

Theorem 4 (Well-defined trades). Let $0 < a < 1$ and $x, y > 0$.
  1. If $\Delta \ge 0$ and (10) holds, the payout (8) is non-negative, the new base reserve is positive, and the new reserves lie on the same curve. Any other payout that leaves a non-negative base reserve on the curve is equal to (8).
  2. If $0 \le \Delta < y$, the cost (9) is non-negative, both new reserves are positive, and the new reserves lie on the same curve. Any other cost that leaves a non-negative base reserve on the curve is equal to (9).
Proof. Existence and positivity were shown above, and solve_rpow shows that the new reserves lie on the curve. For (a), the payout is non-negative because $(y + \Delta)^a \ge y^a$, so $\bigl(k - (y+\Delta)^a\bigr)^{1/a} \le (k - y^a)^{1/a} = x$. Uniqueness in both parts is eq_solve: a non-negative reserve on the curve must equal (7). $\square$

Uniqueness holds among outcomes that leave a non-negative reserve. Without that restriction the statement would involve Mathlib's values of $x^a$ for negative $x$ (Section 3), which have no meaning here.

5.3Monotonicity (Theorem 2)

Buying fyToken from the pool should make it more expensive, and selling fyToken to the pool should make it cheaper.

Theorem 2 (Monotonicity). Let $t > 0$. A trade that buys a positive amount of fyToken strictly raises the price $(x/y)^t$, and a trade that sells a positive amount strictly lowers it. This holds for each of the four trades in Table 3, on any of the curves.
Proof. Buying fyToken increases $x$ and decreases $y$, so $x/y$ increases, and $b \mapsto b^t$ is strictly increasing on $[0, \infty)$ when $t > 0$. Selling does the opposite. It remains to know that a trade of positive size really moves both reserves in those directions, and keeps them positive. That is Theorem 4, together with the lemmas outGivenIn_pos and inGivenOut_pos, which say that a trade of positive size changes both reserves. $\square$

The price in Theorem 2 is the fee-free marginal price $(x/y)^t$, while the trade itself may use any of the curves. The fee-adjusted marginal prices $(x/y)^{gt}$ and $(x/y)^{t/g}$ are also increasing in $x/y$, so the same argument applies to them.

In price_lt_after_buyFY, the term (inGivenOut_spec ha hx hΔ.le hΔy).1 takes the first part of Theorem 4, that the cost is non-negative. hΔ.le turns $0 < \Delta$ into $0 \le \Delta$.

5.4Path independence (Theorem 6)

The invariant is recomputed from the reserves before every trade. One might therefore expect that splitting a trade into pieces changes the result. It does not. The first piece ends on the curve it started on, so the second piece computes the same $k$.

Theorem 6 (Path independence). Let $0 < a < 1$ and $\Delta_1, \Delta_2 \ge 0$, and suppose the combined sale is possible: $(y + \Delta_1 + \Delta_2)^a \le k$. Selling $\Delta_1$ and then $\Delta_2$ pays the same total as selling $\Delta_1 + \Delta_2$ at once. The same holds for trades with a fixed output, when $\Delta_1 \le y$.
Proof. Let $x_1 = \bigl(k - (y + \Delta_1)^a\bigr)^{1/a}$ be the base reserve after the first sale. Then $x_1^a + (y + \Delta_1)^a = k$, so the second sale computes the same invariant $k$ and leaves the base reserve at $x_2 = \bigl(k - (y + \Delta_1 + \Delta_2)^a\bigr)^{1/a}$. The total paid is $(x - x_1) + (x_1 - x_2) = x - x_2$, which is (8) for a single sale of $\Delta_1 + \Delta_2$. $\square$

In the running pool, selling $4$ fyToken pays ${{split4}}$ base, and selling $6$ more then pays ${{split6}}$. The total, ${{splitsum}}$, is the payout for selling $10$ at once.

Since the proof works for any exponent $a$, path independence also holds on the fee curves, as long as the pieces go in the same direction. The paper points this out as an advantage over fees charged as a percentage of the amount sent in, as in Uniswap, where splitting a trade changes the total fee (paper, end of §5). Pieces in opposite directions use different curves. That is the round trip of Section 5.8.

The Lean proof follows the informal one. hk shows that the intermediate reserves are valid, hx1 names the intermediate base reserve, and the rewrite rw [inv_solve ha hk] is the step “the second sale computes the same $k$”.

5.5Scale invariance (Theorem 8)

Suppose every reserve is multiplied by $\lambda > 0$, as happens when liquidity is added in proportion to the existing reserves. The invariant does not scale by $\lambda$:

$$(\lambda x)^a + (\lambda y)^a = \lambda^a \left(x^a + y^a\right) = \lambda^{1-t} k.$$

Trades still scale in the simplest possible way, provided the trade size scales too.

Theorem 8 (Scale invariance). Let $\lambda > 0$. Multiplying both reserves and the trade size by $\lambda$ multiplies the result of every trade by $\lambda$, for trades with a fixed input (when they are possible) and with a fixed output. The price and the rate do not change.
Proof. In (7), $$\bigl(\lambda^a k - (\lambda z)^a\bigr)^{1/a} = \bigl(\lambda^a (k - z^a)\bigr)^{1/a} = \lambda\,(k - z^a)^{1/a}.$$ So every reserve after the scaled trade is $\lambda$ times the corresponding reserve after the original trade, and so is the difference between the reserves before and after. The price depends only on $\lambda x / \lambda y = x / y$. $\square$

A pool with reserves $(1000, 1100)$ pays ${{scale100}}$ base for $100$ fyToken, ten times the ${{out10}}$ that the pool $(100, 110)$ pays for $10$. If only the reserves are scaled and the trade size stays fixed, there is no relation this simple.

The hypothesis hv keeps $k - (y + \Delta)^a$ non-negative. It is needed because the step $(\lambda^a c)^{1/a} = \lambda\, c^{1/a}$, Mathlib's mul_rpow, requires both factors to be non-negative.

5.6A change of variables

The remaining proofs about prices use one idea twice. Write

$$u = x^a, \qquad w = y^a.$$

In these variables the invariant $u + w = k$ is a straight line, and every trade moves along it. If a trade raises $w$ by $d$, it lowers $u$ by exactly $d$. This is the step at the heart of the paper's derivation in Appendix C, where integrating $y^{-t}\,dy = -x^{-t}\,dx$ says that $y^{1-t}$ and $x^{1-t}$ change by equal and opposite amounts. The reserves themselves are recovered with

$$\psi(s) = s^{1/a},$$

which is increasing and, because $1/a > 1$, strictly convex. A sale of $\Delta$ fyToken is therefore described by two intervals of the same width $d$: the base interval $[u', u]$, which $u$ moves across, and the fyToken interval $[w, w']$, which $w$ moves across. The amounts that change hands are the increases of $\psi$ across them:

$$\text{out} = \psi(u) - \psi(u'), \qquad \Delta = \psi(w') - \psi(w). \tag{12}$$

In the running example $a = 1/2$ and $\psi(s) = s^2$. Selling $10$ fyToken moves $w$ from $\sqrt{110} = {{sqrt110}}$ to $\sqrt{120} = {{sqrt120}}$, so $d = {{d10}}$, and moves $u$ from $10$ down to ${{u1_10}}$. Then $\Delta = {{sqrt120}}^2 - {{sqrt110}}^2 = 10$ and $\text{out} = 10^2 - {{u1_10}}^2 = {{out10}}$.

Because $\psi$ is convex, its increase over an interval of fixed width is larger the further right the interval lies. In Figure 2 the base interval lies to the left of the fyToken interval, because $x < y$, and so the trader receives less base than the fyToken they send. The next two sections make this precise.

5.7Price impact (Theorem 7)

The average price of a trade is the amount of base exchanged per fyToken, $\text{out}/\Delta$ for a sale. The difference between the average price and the marginal price before the trade is the trade's price impact. We want to show that price impact always works against the trader, and grows with the size of the trade. In the running example, selling $10$ fyToken happens at an average price of ${{avg10}}$. That lies between the marginal price before the trade, ${{price0}}$, and the marginal price after it, $({{x1_10}}/120)^{0.5} = {{post10}}$. Figure 3 shows the same fact on the curve, for a larger sale.

Theorem 7 (Price impact). Let $0 < a < 1$ and $\tau = 1 - a$, let $x, y, \Delta > 0$, let the sale of $\Delta$ be possible, and let $\text{out}$ be its payout (8). Then $$\left(\frac{x - \text{out}}{y + \Delta}\right)^{\tau} \;<\; \frac{\text{out}}{\Delta} \;<\; \left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{\tau}.$$ For a purchase of $\Delta < y$ the average price likewise lies strictly between the marginal price before the trade (now the lower bound) and the marginal price after it. The average price of a sale strictly decreases as the sale grows, and the average price of a purchase strictly increases.

Proof. Use the variables of Section 5.6. By the mean value theorem there are $\xi \in (u', u)$ and $\eta \in (w, w')$ with

$$\psi(u) - \psi(u') = \psi'(\xi)\, d, \qquad \psi(w') - \psi(w) = \psi'(\eta)\, d.$$

Since $\psi'(s) = \tfrac{1}{a}\, s^{1/a - 1}$, dividing the first equation by the second and using (12) gives

$$\frac{\text{out}}{\Delta} = \left(\frac{\xi}{\eta}\right)^{1/a - 1}. \tag{13}$$

The marginal prices can be written in the same variables. Since $x = u^{1/a}$, $y = w^{1/a}$ and $\tau / a = 1/a - 1$,

$$\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{\tau} = \left(\frac{u}{w}\right)^{1/a - 1},$$

and likewise the marginal price after the trade is $(u'/w')^{1/a - 1}$. Now $u' < \xi < u$ and $w < \eta < w'$ give

$$\frac{u'}{w'} \;<\; \frac{\xi}{\eta} \;<\; \frac{u}{w},$$

and raising to the positive power $1/a - 1$ gives the inequalities for a sale.

A purchase is a sale run backwards. Buying $\Delta$ from $(x, y)$ ends at $(x + \text{in}, y - \Delta)$, and selling $\Delta$ from there returns to $(x, y)$ and pays exactly $\text{in}$ (the lemma outGivenIn_reverse). Applying the result for sales at the later reserves gives the result for purchases.

For the last claim, take $0 < \Delta_1 < \Delta_2$, and use Theorem 6 to split the larger sale into $\Delta_1$ followed by $\Delta_2 - \Delta_1$. The second piece starts where the first one ends. By what we just proved, its average price is below the marginal price at that point, which is below the average price of the first piece. The average price of the whole sale is a weighted average of the two pieces' average prices, so it is below that of the first piece alone. The argument for purchases is the same. $\square$

For a purchase from the running pool the order is reversed. Buying $5$ fyToken costs ${{buy5}}$ base, an average price of ${{buy5avg}}$, between ${{price0}}$ before the trade and ${{buy5post}}$ after it.

Mathlib's mean value theorem, exists_hasDerivAt_eq_slope, needs the function to be continuous on the closed interval and differentiable inside it. The lemma hasDerivAt_rpow_const supplies the derivative $p\, z^{p - 1}$ of $z \mapsto z^p$.

On the fee curves, $\tau$ is $gt$ or $t/g$, so the prices that bracket the average are the fee-adjusted marginal prices. The fee-free versions, avgPrice_sellFY_noFee and avgPrice_buyFY_noFee, state the result with price t.

5.8Round trips (Theorem 3)

Suppose a trader sells $\Delta$ fyToken and then immediately buys $\Delta$ fyToken back. Without a fee both trades use the same curve, and the trader should end where they started. With a fee, the sale uses the curve with exponent $\alpha = 1 - t/g$, which we call the $\alpha$-curve, and the purchase uses the curve with exponent $\beta = 1 - gt$, the $\beta$-curve. When $g < 1$, $\alpha < \beta$. We want the fee to make every round trip cost the trader something.

Theorem 3 (No free round trips).
  1. Without a fee, a round trip breaks even: buying $\Delta$ back costs exactly what selling $\Delta$ paid.
  2. Let $g < 1$, $0 < x \le y$ and $\Delta > 0$, and let the sale be possible. Then buying $\Delta$ back costs strictly more than selling $\Delta$ paid.

Part (b) needs the hypothesis $x \le y$, and without it the statement is false. We return to this after the proof.

Proof. (a) The sale leaves reserves $(x_1, y + \Delta)$ on the curve through $(x, y)$. Buying $\Delta$ back on the same curve leads to the point of that curve with fyToken reserve $y$, and by the uniqueness in Theorem 4 that point is $(x, y)$. So the purchase costs $x - x_1$, which is what the sale paid.

(b) The sale leaves reserves $(x_1, y + \Delta)$ on the $\alpha$-curve. Use the variables of Section 5.6 with the exponent $\alpha$:

$$u' = x_1^{\alpha}, \quad u = x^{\alpha}, \quad w = y^{\alpha}, \quad w' = (y + \Delta)^{\alpha}.$$

As before, $[u', u]$ and $[w, w']$ have the same width. The purchase uses the $\beta$-curve through $(x_1, y + \Delta)$, and ends at a point $(x_2, y)$ with $x_2^{\beta} + y^{\beta} = x_1^{\beta} + (y + \Delta)^{\beta}$. It costs $x_2 - x_1$, and the sale paid $x - x_1$, so the purchase costs more exactly when $x_2 > x$. Raising both sides to the power $\beta$ and substituting for $x_2^{\beta}$,

$$x_2 > x \iff x_1^{\beta} + (y + \Delta)^{\beta} - y^{\beta} > x^{\beta} \iff x^{\beta} - x_1^{\beta} \;<\; (y + \Delta)^{\beta} - y^{\beta}. \tag{14}$$

Write each $\beta$-power through an $\alpha$-power, $z^{\beta} = (z^{\alpha})^{\beta/\alpha}$, and let $\varphi(s) = s^{\beta/\alpha}$. Then (14) reads

$$\varphi(u) - \varphi(u') \;<\; \varphi(w') - \varphi(w).$$

Since $\beta/\alpha > 1$, $\varphi$ is strictly convex, so its increase over an interval of fixed width grows as the interval moves right. The two intervals have the same width, and $u' < u \le w$ because $x_1 < x \le y$. So the inequality holds. $\square$

In the running pool with $g = 0.95$, selling $10$ fyToken pays ${{rt_sell}}$ base and buying $10$ back costs ${{rt_buy}}$. The trader loses ${{rt_loss}}$.

Where the hypothesis comes from. The argument used only that the base interval $[u', u]$ lies to the left of the fyToken interval $[w, w']$, that is, $x_1 < y$. If instead $x_1 > y$, the base interval lies to the right, the inequality reverses, and the round trip pays the trader. When $x_1 = y$ the two intervals coincide and the round trip breaks even. Figure 5 shows this for $y = 100$, $\Delta = 10$, $t = 0.5$ and $g = 0.95$. The trader's result changes sign at exactly $x = 110$, where the sale leaves $x_1 = 100 = y$. For example, starting from $(x, y) = (200, 100)$ the sale pays ${{rt2_sell}}$ and buying back costs ${{rt2_buy}}$, a gain of ${{rt2_gain}}$.

This agrees with a remark in the paper (Appendix E): when the interest rate is negative, that is, $x > y$, the fee benefits the trader rather than the pool. The paper notes that such reserves can arise if someone sends base tokens directly to the pool. The Lean theorem assumes $x \le y$, which implies $x_1 < y$. Only this direction is formalized.

The convexity step uses Mathlib's StrictConvexOn.slope_strict_mono_adjacent: for a strictly convex function and points $p < q < r$, the slope over $[p, q]$ is less than the slope over $[q, r]$. When the intervals touch ($u = w$) one application suffices. When they are apart, two applications compare $[u', u]$ with $[u, w]$, and then $[u, w]$ with $[w, w']$.

In roundTrip_lt, hconv is the convexity of $\varphi$, hpow is the identity $(z^{\alpha})^{\beta/\alpha} = z^{\beta}$, hlen says the two intervals have the same width, and hinc is (14), obtained from increment_lt and rewritten with hpow. hlt is the statement $x < x_2$. The theorem about the fee trades is then a direct application:

5.9Convergence at maturity (Theorem 5)

At maturity a fyToken is worth exactly one unit of base, so the pool should trade one for one. Every exponent in use, $1 - t$, $1 - gt$ and $1 - t/g$, tends to $1$ as $t \to 0$, and at exponent $1$ the invariant is the constant sum $x + y = k$.

Theorem 5 (Convergence at maturity). Fix reserves $x, y$ and a trade size $\Delta$. As $t \to 0$:
  1. for each $z$, the point of the curve through $(x, y)$ with fyToken reserve $z$ has a base reserve that tends to $x + y - z$, the value on the constant-sum line;
  2. the result of each of the four trades in Table 3 tends to $\Delta$;
  3. the price $(x/y)^t$ tends to $1$.
Proof. For (a) it suffices to show that $a \mapsto (x^a + y^a - z^a)^{1/a}$ is continuous at $a = 1$, because then its limit is its value there, $x + y - z$. Each $a \mapsto c^a$ is continuous at $a = 1$, so is $a \mapsto 1/a$, and $(b, e) \mapsto b^e$ is continuous at every point where $e > 0$. At $a = 1$ the exponent $1/a$ is $1$, so the pieces combine. Each trade result is $x$ minus such a value, or such a value minus $x$, which gives (b). For (c), $t \mapsto (x/y)^t$ is continuous and equals $1$ at $t = 0$. $\square$

In Mathlib, limits are written with filters. Tendsto f (𝓝 1) (𝓝 L) means that $f(a) \to L$ as $a \to 1$, where 𝓝 1 stands for the neighborhoods of $1$. The project proves continuity in the exponent once, and then composes it with any path of exponents e that tends to $1$, along any filter l. The same lemma therefore gives the fee-free result and the results with fees.

Mathlib's lemma ContinuousAt.rpow requires either a nonzero base or a positive exponent. At $a = 1$ the exponent is positive, so no hypothesis on the reserves is needed. The convergence is pointwise: it holds for each fixed trade, and is not claimed to be uniform over all trades.

6Where the formalization and the paper differ

Writing each property precisely enough for Lean to check it raised the following points.

Two notions of rate

The paper's rate, $r = y/x - 1$ in (5), is a rate per unit of scaled time; with $t = 1$ at four years it is a four-year rate. The return to maturity, $(y/x)^t - 1$ in (4), is a different number. Both are defined in the project, and one_add_rate relates them.

Round trips at negative rates

“A round trip with a fee loses money” holds when $x \le y$, and fails when the sale leaves more base than the pool's starting fyToken (Section 5.8, Figure 5). The paper mentions this for negative rates in Appendix E. Theorem 3 carries the hypothesis $x \le y$.

A limit on trades with a fixed input

The constant power sum curve meets both axes, so a sale of fyToken is possible only while $(y + \Delta)^{1-t} < k$ (Section 5.2). The constant product has no such limit, and the paper does not discuss it. A sale of at most $x$ fyToken is always possible.

The worked example in §6.3

The paper's example sells $100$ fyDai into a pool with $100$ Dai and $100$ virtual fyDai, at $t = 0.5$ with $g = 0.95$, and reports $x = 34.31$, so that ${{paper_nofee}}$ Dai is paid out. That is the result on the fee-free curve, exponent $0.5$. On the selling curve the text describes, exponent $1 - t/g = {{a_sell}}$, the base reserve becomes ${{paper_fee_x}}$ and the payout is ${{paper_fee}}$ Dai.

Path independence with fees

The paper's remark that splitting a trade does not change the fee paid (end of §5) is proved here (Theorem 6) for pieces in the same direction. Pieces in opposite directions form a round trip, which is covered by Theorem 3.

Which curve each trade uses

The paper assigns curves by direction, buying or selling fyToken. Table 3 extends this to trades where the trader fixes the amount of base.

What is not formalized

Liquidity tokens and the accounting for virtual reserves (paper §6), the proofs about the share invariant (Appendices E and F), and the limit $t \to 1$ (Appendix D). In this project $y$ is simply the fyToken reserve used in the formula, virtual reserves included.
